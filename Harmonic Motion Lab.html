Derivation: from forces to g = 4π² ÷ gradient
  1. The bob (mass m) hangs on a string of length l, displaced by angle θ. Gravity's component along the arc pulls it back: F = −mg sin θ
  2. The bob moves along an arc of length s = lθ, so its acceleration along the arc is l d²θdt². Newton's second law, F = ma: ml d²θdt² = −mg sin θ
  3. Divide both sides by ml. The mass cancels, which is why the bob's mass does not matter: d²θdt² = −gl sin θ
  4. For small angles (in radians) sin θ = θ − θ³/6 + … ≈ θ. At 10° the error is under 0.6%, so: d²θdt² = −gl θ This has the form d²θdt² = −ω²θ, the equation of simple harmonic motion, with ω² = g/l.
  5. Its solution is θ = θ0 cos(ωt). One full cycle takes ωT = 2π: T = 2πω = 2π lg
  6. Square both sides so the relationship becomes a straight line through the origin: T² = 4π²g l Plotting T² (y) against l (x) gives gradient m = 4π²/g.
  7. Rearranging gives gravity from the graph: g = 4π²m
  8. Project numbers, reading two points from the graph: m = 3.36 − 2.920.80 − 0.70 = 4.40 s²/m g = 4π²4.40 = 39.4784.40 = 8.97 m/s² error = 9.81 − 8.979.81 × 100 = 8.56%

When the small-angle model stops being safe

The formula T = 2π√(l/g) assumes sin θ ≈ θ. Release the pendulum wider and the real period grows, so the formula under-reports g. Here the exact period is computed, and the project's 70° trial is placed on it.

Presenting this screen
  • At 70° the real period is about 10% longer than the formula says. Using the formula anyway makes g read about 18% low.
  • The error budget bar splits the 23.66% error into physics we can predict and error left over for timing and length.
  • Takeaway for design: any linear vibration model carries an amplitude limit, and it can be quantified.

Derivation: the exact period of a pendulum
  1. Use energy conservation instead of force. Released from rest at angle θ0, the bob has dropped by l(cos θ − cos θ0) at angle θ: ½ ml²(dθdt)² = mgl (cos θ − cos θ0)
  2. Solve for the angular speed: dθdt = (2g/l)(cos θ − cos θ0)
  3. Use cos θ = 1 − 2 sin²(θ/2). The 1's cancel in the difference: cos θ − cos θ0 = 2[sin²(θ0/2) − sin²(θ/2)] dθdt = 2 g/l sin²(θ0/2) − sin²(θ/2)
  4. The bob takes a quarter period to go from θ = 0 to θ0, so T = 4 ∫ dθ ÷ (dθdt): T = 2 l/g ∫0θ₀ dθsin²(θ0/2) − sin²(θ/2)
  5. Substitute sin(θ/2) = k sin φ with k = sin(θ0/2). Then θ = 0 gives φ = 0 and θ = θ0 gives φ = π/2. The square-root term becomes: sin²(θ0/2) − sin²(θ/2) = k² − k² sin²φ = k² cos²φ
  6. Differentiate the substitution: ½ cos(θ/2) dθ = k cos φ dφ, and cos(θ/2) = √(1 − k² sin²φ), so: dθ = 2k cos φ dφ1 − k² sin²φ
  7. Put both pieces into the integral. The factors k cos φ cancel top and bottom, and the 2 outside cancels the 2 in dθ: T = 4 l/g ∫0π/2 dφ1 − k² sin²φ = 4 l/g K(k) K(k) is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. This page evaluates it with the arithmetic–geometric mean.
  8. Check the limit. For small k, K = (π/2)(1 + k²/4 + 9k⁴/64 + …) and k ≈ θ0/2, which gives: T ≈ 2π l/g (1 + θ0²/16 + 11θ0⁴/3072 + …) At small angles this returns the 2π√(l/g) used on the previous screen.
  9. Project numbers at θ0 = 70°, l = 1.00 m: k = sin 35° = 0.5736, 2K(k)/π = 1.1021, so T = 2.006 × 1.1021 = 2.211 s. The small-angle formula gives 2.006 s.

Arc length against straight-line displacement

Simple harmonic motion needs the restoring force to scale with displacement. For small swings the curved path (arc) and the straight chord between the two ends are almost the same length. Trigonometry lets us measure exactly how much they differ.

Does motion make a hot object lose heat faster?

A hot bob was left still or swung in air for 40 seconds. Scrub time to see both cool, then see how the same data reads under Newton's law of cooling.

Presenting this screen
  • Moving bob: 16.33 °C lost. Still bob: 9.00 °C. That is 81.44% more.
  • The cooling-rate constant k removes the effect of the different start temperatures and lands near 1.7 times. Both views agree that motion helps.
  • Airflow past a surface is what a fan, a heat-sink or a heat exchanger provides.
